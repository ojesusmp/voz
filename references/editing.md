# Editing a draft someone else wrote

Load this when a draft already exists and it is not yours, or when the user asks you to flag, audit, or scan a piece rather than rewrite it.

The rest of `voz` teaches you to write. This file teaches you to leave things alone.

## Whose voice wins

The draft is theirs. Edit toward their voice with the tells removed, never toward yours.

Read the whole draft before you change anything, and name its core point to yourself. If you cannot find the core point, ask, because everything after this depends on knowing it.

Then inventory what makes it sound like a person: vocabulary, cadence, bluntness, humor, hedges, digressions, and level of polish. A rough draft with a real voice should still sound like the same person afterwards.

Keep, on purpose:

- Strong opinions and blunt wording. Profanity, if that is how they talk.
- Self-interruptions, asides, and detours that carry character.
- Honest admissions. "I shipped it and it broke" is worth more than any polish you could add.
- "I think", "maybe", "to be honest" when they carry real uncertainty or the writer's spoken rhythm. In your own prose you hedge once and move on. In theirs, a hedge can be the point.
- Humor, including the joke that does not quite land.
- One well-placed fragment. One short paragraph. A sentence that starts with "And".

The test: would the writer read it back and recognise it as theirs? If they would think an editor had been through it, you cut too much.

## Minimum effective edit

Fix the tells, the errors, the repetition, and the passages that are hard to follow. Then stop.

- Leave strong human sentences alone. Do not rewrite a good line for consistency.
- Cut in proportion to the slop. A clean draft with two tells gets two changes, not a polish pass.
- Do not tidy every paragraph to the same finish. Uniform polish is its own tell.
- Keep their structure and their detours unless the structure is hurting the piece. If you reorder anything, say so and say why.
- Front-load the point only where it helps this reader. Do not force every section into the same shape.
- Untangle sentences that are actually confusing. Leave the long spoken sentence that is clear and characteristic. A long sentence can be perfectly clear, so split the tangled ones and leave the rest alone.

## Never invent

No claims, examples, numbers, quotes, sources, or opinions that the draft did not contain.

- Weasel attribution ("experts agree", "studies show") gets flagged or queried, never filled in with a source you supplied.
- Protect the specific fact. If the draft says "cut review time from 30 minutes to 8", that number survives. Smoothing it into "significantly faster" destroys the best thing in the sentence. Vagueness is the direction slop travels, and an editor can push a draft that way without noticing.
- If something is unclear and the meaning matters, ask. Do not guess and write it down.

And never alter, even to improve it: anything inside quotation marks, code, command output, a proper name, a citation, a URL, a figure, or a legal term of art. Smoothing a sentence somebody else said changes a fact exactly as much as changing a number does, and "indemnify" is not a fancy way of saying "pay". If one of those reads badly, say so in What changed and leave it alone.

## Kill-list words in a draft you did not write

Same posture as everywhere else in `voz`: probabilities, not laws. Replace a flagged word when it sits in a cluster or is a strong tell. Keep it when it is the writer's precise word and the paragraph around it is clean. Then say which ones you kept, so the choice is visible rather than silent.

## The one override

House rule: zero em dashes, including in a draft that arrived full of them. This is the single place `voz` changes a writer's punctuation on principle rather than because it is a tell. Use a comma, a colon, parentheses, or two sentences, and note it in What changed so the writer can see it was deliberate and reverse it if they want.

## Ask only when the answer changes the edit

- No draft: ask them to paste it.
- Audience or format unclear: one question. "Who is this for, and where will it be published?"
- Goal unclear: "What should the reader think, feel, or do after reading this?"

Otherwise infer it and go. Three questions before starting is its own kind of throat-clearing, and in an unattended run there is nobody to answer. State the assumption in What changed instead and carry on.

## Output contract

Return the full edited draft, then a short **What changed** note.

Open that note with the voice you set out to keep, in three to five words: "blunt, first person, jokes, one-word beats". Writing it down forces the inventory to actually happen instead of being assumed, and it gives the writer something concrete to disagree with. If your description of their voice is wrong, they will tell you, and that is worth more than any edit in the list below it.

One line per pattern class you touched: the name, roughly how many, and one quoted example. Add any reordering and the reason. Add the em dash note if it applied. Add any flagged word you deliberately kept.

Keep it short enough that the writer can find and reverse any single change.

## Audit mode: flag, do not fix

When the user asks whether a piece is slop, or asks you to audit, scan, or flag it without rewriting, switch to this and stop there.

Report shape, one line per finding:

`pattern name | the quoted line | the fix in a few words`

Then, once, this disclaimer: **these are patterns in the text, not a verdict on its author.**

Do not rewrite. Do not score it out of ten. Do not say whether a machine wrote it, and do not imply it. A detector guesses; a named pattern is evidence the reader can check for themselves, which is the whole reason this mode is worth having. Offer to edit afterwards.

Naming what is in someone's draft helps them. Announcing that a machine wrote it does not, and that is the part to refuse.

## Post-edit checks

Run these on the edited draft. Each is pass or fail. Fix and rerun until they all pass.

**Did you keep the person?**
1. Their point survives, and nothing was invented: no added claim, number, example, or opinion.
2. Voice traits survive: vocabulary, cadence, bluntness, humor, real hedges, digressions.
3. Strong sentences were left alone rather than rewritten for consistency.
4. The amount cut is proportional to the actual slop.
5. Their structure survives, or a reorder is explained.
6. Every specific fact from the draft is still there, still specific.
7. It leads with what this reader needs, and any personal setup carrying context, tension, or character is still in place.
8. Anything front-loaded was front-loaded because it helped, not because every section was pushed into the same shape.
9. Tangled sentences are fixed, and the spoken cadence, the fragments, and the changes of pace are intact.

**Did you remove the slop?**
13. Kill-list words are gone, or kept on purpose and named.
14. Empty adverbs and empty phrases are gone where they carried nothing.
10. Repeated sentence shapes and identically built paragraphs were varied where varying helped.
11. At most one negative parallelism in the whole piece, and only where it earns the drama.
12. These patterns are gone: binary contrasts, negative listing, throat-clearing openers, faux-insight setups, rhetorical setups, colon reveals, superficial "-ing" tails, importance puffery, weasel attribution, inflated copulas, synonym cycling, stacked dramatic fragments, interpretive metadiscourse, fake-profound kickers, recap endings.
15. Every generic sentence either passed the portability test or was made specific.
16. Human subjects and direct verbs where the draft supports them. No abstraction doing a human verb to hide who acted.
17. Formatting slop gone. Headings in sentence case. Sentence case after a colon is preferred rather than enforced in someone else's draft, because their capitalisation is a style choice and the em dash is the only punctuation voz overrides on principle. No heading over two sentences.
18. No em dashes anywhere.

**Did you add anything while removing?**
19. Re-scan only the sentences you changed, against the kill-list and the pattern list above. Editors introduce tells while removing them. You cut "leverage" and a rule of three appears in its place, or tidying an ending bolts a summary onto it. This check catches your own work, and it is the one most likely to fail.

**Is it finished?**
20. The writer would recognise it as theirs.
21. It would sound natural read aloud to a sharp colleague.
22. The full edited draft and a What changed note are both present.
23. For an audit request: named patterns with quoted lines and short fixes, no rewrite, no score, no claim about authorship, disclaimer present.
